## Highlights


### 1. Engineering conditional peptide self-assembly using AlphaFold
- **Authors:** Helen Futty, Sofia Chinelli, Ravi R. Sonani, Tomás S. Plivelic, Annela Seddon, Edward H. Egelman, Andrew R. Thomson, Dave J. Adams
- **Date / venue:** Cell Reports Physical Science, October 2026 issue (preprint on ChemRxiv, 2026-06-15)
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrp.2026.103551 (open preprint: https://doi.org/10.26434/chemrxiv.15004727/v1)
- **Tidbits:**
  - Uses AlphaFold Multimer confidence scores as a stand-in for "how likely are these peptides to assemble."
  - Designs *pairs* of peptides that stay floppy and unstructured alone but form tape-like nanostructures only when mixed.
  - The trick is selecting for co-assembly **and** against self-assembly of each component at the same time.
  - Validated with circular dichroism, small-angle X-ray scattering, and cryo-EM.
  - Limitation stated by the authors: AlphaFold predicts molecular packing, not how sheets stack into bigger structures.
- **Why a beginner should care:** It's a clear example of "design the parts so the structure only appears when the right partners meet," which is the core idea behind programmable self-assembly.
- **Media:** Cryo-EM / SAXS figures in the paper.

### 2. Migrating cell clusters as active droplets with asymmetric shape and contact angles
- **Authors:** Hanno I. Hennighausen, Jaime A. Espina, Elias H. Barriga, Ricard Alert
- **Date / venue:** arXiv preprint, submitted 2026-10-02 (cond-mat.soft / physics.bio-ph)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03536
- **Tidbits:**
  - Clusters of frog neural crest cells migrate together, and the model treats them as droplets that "wet" the surface.
  - Experiments show the clusters are lopsided: a lower contact angle at the front than at the back.
  - Asymmetric pulling forces create a pressure gradient inside the tissue, which (via the Young–Laplace law) bends the top surface unevenly.
  - Fitting the shape lets them estimate the tissue's viscosity from a height profile alone.
- **Why a beginner should care:** Living groups of cells can be understood with the same surface-tension physics as a raindrop on a window.

### 3. Decentralized power-optimal coordination for spacecraft swarms using magnetorquers
- **Authors:** Yuta Takahashi, Shin-ichiro Sakai
- **Date / venue:** arXiv preprint, submitted 2026-10-01 (cs.RO / eess.SY)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02118
- **Tidbits:**
  - Swarms of small spacecraft could hold the shape of a structure larger than any single rocket can launch.
  - Magnetic coils (magnetorquers) let them push and pull on each other with no propellant, using solar power.
  - Every craft affects every other in range, so they split interactions across carrier frequencies and solve it decentrally.
  - A closed-loop simulation with a thousand spacecraft confirms the controller.
- **Why a beginner should care:** It's "modular robots assembling a structure" scaled up to orbit, and it shows why local rules plus coordination matter.
- **Caveat:** Simulation only.

### 4. Toward controlling biology with language: prompt-conditioned interventions for cells, organoids, and biobots
- **Authors:** Nam H. Le, Douglas Blackiston, Michael Levin, Josh Bongard
- **Date / venue:** arXiv preprint, submitted 2026-09-30
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02247
- **Tidbits:** Learns a natural-language-to-intervention mapping for xenobots entirely from an archive of past experiments, using a vision-language model as the judge; 80.0% held-out accuracy vs a 66.7% chance baseline.
- **Why a beginner should care:** Xenobots are self-organizing multicellular constructs; this asks whether you can "describe the behavior you want" instead of hand-designing it.
- **Caveat:** Offline evaluation on archived data; no new wet-lab tests.
