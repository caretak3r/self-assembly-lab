# DNA nanotech

DNA origami tubes, nanodonuts, and DNA-programmed crystals from the digests all start from **two ingredients**: a long "scaffold" strand and many short "staple" strands, plus buffers and a way to heat and cool them.

## What exists today

- **Custom short DNA (oligos)** made to order by synthesis companies and delivered dry or in plates.
- **Oligo pools**: thousands of sequences on one order, cheaper per strand but mixed together.
- **Scaffold strands** such as the M13mp18 single-stranded genome (about 7,249 bases) and longer custom scaffolds.
- **Free design and simulation tools** that let you design and test a structure on a laptop before you buy anything.

## Maturity

- Design tools: **hobbyist** (free, anyone can use them).
- Oligos, scaffolds, and buffers: **university-lab**. Suppliers sell to institutions and usually need an account with a shipping address at a lab.
- Making DNA origami in bulk (grams or more): early **industrial** efforts exist, but it is not a routine commodity.
- DNA nanodevices as medicines: **not yet available**. The lymphoma device in the 2026-10-23 digest was tested in mice.

## Bottlenecks and gaps

- **Cost**: a single origami design needs about 200 staple strands, so a first order is a real budget item.
- **Purification and checking**: you need gels and usually AFM or TEM to see whether it folded. See [Characterization](/supply-chain/characterization).
- **Stability**: origami falls apart in low-salt water or with enzymes around, which limits real-world use.
- **Scale**: making lots of scaffold and staples cheaply (for example by growing them in bacteria or with enzymes) is still being developed.

## What would need to exist for scale-up

Cheap bulk staple production, standard ready-made origami "breadboards" with addressable spots you can buy off the shelf, stabilizing coatings that work outside the lab, and fast quality checks that don't need an electron microscope.

## Safety notes

- Synthetic DNA strands for origami are not living and are not infectious. They still belong in a supervised lab with gloves and proper waste disposal.
- Reputable synthesis companies screen orders for sequences of concern. Many follow the [International Gene Synthesis Consortium](https://genesynthesisconsortium.org/) protocol, so expect to provide institutional details.
- If your work ever involves living cells or bacteria, your institution's biosafety rules apply. In the US these follow the [NIH biosafety policies](https://osp.od.nih.gov/policies/biosafety-and-biosecurity-policy/).
