# Microfabrication and patterned substrates

DNA origami landing spots on a chip, patterned "active stripes", and microscopic folding robots all need **surfaces patterned at the micrometer or nanometer scale**. Nobody owns a cleanroom at home, but access is easier than most students think.

## What exists today

- **Shared university cleanrooms** that rent tool time and training to outside users, including companies.
- **Multi-project wafer (MPW) services** where many customers share one wafer, so each pays only for a small chip area.
- **MEMS foundry services** that build your micro-mechanical design in a standard process.
- **Maskless lithography** (writing patterns directly with a laser) for fast pattern changes without making a photomask.
- **Two-photon 3D printing** for tiny 3D parts with sub-micrometer detail.
- **Microfluidic chip makers** for channels that guide particles and droplets.

## Maturity

- The underlying processes: **industrial** (the chip industry).
- Access for small users: **university-lab**, through shared facilities and MPW runs.
- Patterned "self-assembly templates" as catalog products: **not yet available**.

## Bottlenecks and gaps

- **Lead times**: MPW runs follow a fixed schedule, and chips can take months to arrive.
- **Expertise**: designs must follow each foundry's design rules. Cleanroom tools need hours of training.
- **Mixing DNA or biology with chips** is not a standard foundry process. Labs do that last step themselves.

## What would need to exist for scale-up

Standard "assembly-ready" patterned substrates (spots, stripes, wells) you can order like a microscope slide, foundry processes that are compatible with DNA and proteins, and faster small-batch turnaround.

## Safety notes

Cleanrooms use hydrofluoric acid, strong solvents, and toxic gases. Only trained users work there, and students usually participate through a supervised program or outreach day. Some advanced fabrication tools and technical data are covered by [export controls](https://www.bis.gov/).
