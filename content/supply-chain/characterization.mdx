# Characterization access

You can't tell if something self-assembled correctly without **looking at it**. For the nanometer-scale work in the digests, that means microscopes most people will never own.

## What exists today

- **Optical microscopes** (including phone-based ones) for anything above about a micrometer: colloids, droplets, bacteria, robot swarms.
- **Shared university core facilities** offering AFM (atomic force microscopy), SEM, and TEM by the hour, often with training or staff-run service.
- **National user facilities** that give free access for research that will be published, through short proposals.
- **Gel electrophoresis** as the cheap first check for DNA structures.

## Maturity

- Optical microscopy and gels: **hobbyist** to **university-lab**.
- AFM, SEM, TEM: **university-lab**, through shared facilities.
- Fast, cheap, inline checking of nanoscale assembly: **not yet available**.

## Bottlenecks and gaps

Electron microscopy is slow, expensive per sample, and needs trained operators. Most facilities need a sponsoring lab or teacher. Image analysis of thousands of structures is still largely custom code.

## What would need to exist for scale-up

Optical or electrical tests that confirm correct assembly in seconds, automated image analysis, and outreach programs that let high-school and undergraduate students send samples to shared facilities.

## Safety notes

Microscopes are safe to use. Sample prep sometimes uses stains (for example uranyl compounds for TEM) that only trained staff should handle.
