# Manufacturing — how people actually build related hardware today

There is **no mass-market factory for programmable matter**. What exists today is a ladder of *starter paths*: makerspaces, university labs, small-batch PCB houses, 3D printers, and oligo synthesis services.

<Callout type="warning" emoji="⚗️">
This page teaches **how to begin building related machinery and kits** — modules, connectors, electronics, educational molecular workflows — not how to industrialize self-assembling consumer products that do not exist yet.
</Callout>

## The real ladder (idea → prototype → small batch)

```
Idea / sketch
  → CAD + local rules on paper or in simulation
  → Single module prototype (print / laser / breadboard)
  → 5–20 unit small batch (iterate connectors + firmware)
  → Optional PCB revision via a fab house
  → Document BOM + open designs
```

Skip “scale to millions” fantasies until you can make **ten** reliable units.

## Mechanical modules (3D print & laser-cut)

**What beginners actually do:**

1. Design a brick/cube/tile in FreeCAD, Fusion, or Onshape.
2. Add pockets for **magnets**, screws, or snap fits.
3. Print in PLA/PETG on a school/makerspace FDM printer.
4. Or laser-cut acrylic/plywood tiles for 2D capillary/magnet demos.

**Tips that matter for self-assembly toys:**

- Tolerance magnets carefully (press-fit vs glue).
- Chamfer edges so modules can slide into contact.
- Design **polarity** (N/S faces) into the CAD, not as an afterthought.
- Print a 1-unit test before a 20-unit batch.

Open starting points: modular robot papers’ supplementary CAD when released; classroom floatile repos such as [FloaTiles](https://github.com/karegeo/floatiles); your own Exp 7 modules from [Experiments](/experiments).

## Connectors people use in labs

| Connector | Pros | Cons | Typical use |
|-----------|------|------|-------------|
| Permanent neodymium magnets | Cheap, passive | Pinch hazard; hard to undock | M-Block-like toys, tiles |
| Electro-permanent magnets | Controllable dock/undock | Drive electronics needed | SMORES-class research |
| Mechanical latches / hooks | Strong | Wear, alignment critical | Larger modular robots |
| Velcro / elastomer friction | Safe classroom | Low precision | Soft demos |
| Capillary / wetting patches | No electronics | Wet mess; scale limits | Floatiles |

## Electronics path (Arduino / ESP32)

Most student swarm prototypes are “a brain + radios/IR + motors + battery” repeated N times.

**Typical student stack:**

- MCU: Arduino Uno/Nano, ESP32, or STM32 Nucleo
- Motion: vibration motors, small DC gearmotors, or servos
- Sensing: IR reflectance, ToF, bump switches, IMU
- Comms: UART daisy-chain, ESP-NOW, BLE, or IR like Kilobots
- Power: LiPo with proper charging protection (makerspace supervision)

Firmware pattern: **sense neighbors → apply local rule → actuate**. Keep the rule short enough to explain on one sticky note.

## PCB fabrication (small batch, not a fab)

You do not etch boards at home for serious revision control. Students usually:

1. Draw schematic + PCB in KiCad (free).
2. Export Gerbers.
3. Order from a prototype house (examples people actually use: **JLCPCB**, PCBWay, OSH Park).
4. Optionally order **PCBA** (assembly) for a few boards when the BOM is simple.

Watch a real factory tour to calibrate expectations (this is *how boards are made*, not a programmable-matter plant):

Cost realism (order-of-magnitude, 2020s hobbyist): bare 2-layer boards often a few dollars each at quantity 5–10; assembled boards cost more once parts + stencil + labor are included.

## Kilobot & open modular lineages

- **Kilobots** — designed for automated assembly and low cost per unit for *research swarms*. Academic/commercial kit availability changes over time; check [Harvard SSR Kilobots page](https://ssr.seas.harvard.edu/kilobots) and educational vendors. Many learners start with **simulators** first.
- **M-Blocks / SMORES / other MRR** — research hardware; learn from papers and lab pages ([SMORES-EP](https://www.modlabupenn.org/smores-ep/), MIT M-Blocks videos) rather than expecting Amazon kits.
- **DIY vibration robots** — feasible makerspace project inspired by Kilobot locomotion (not a clone of the research platform).

## Molecular work: DNA as a *service*

You generally **do not** synthesize DNA oligos yourself as a beginner.

1. Design sequences (paper, cadnano, or lab software).
2. Order oligos / gene fragments from vendors (commonly cited educational/research vendors include **IDT**, Twist, etc.).
3. Fold / anneal in a supervised wet lab with buffers, thermal cycler, and gel/AFM access.
4. Image via university **core facilities**.

Home “DNA origami kits” that promise kitchen nanotech should be treated with extreme skepticism. See [Supply chain](/supply-chain) for educational reagent boundaries.

## Safety & cost realism

| Topic | Reality check |
|-------|----------------|
| Magnets | Pinch injuries; keep &gt; strong magnets away from implants |
| LiPo batteries | Fire risk if punctured/overcharged — use proper chargers |
| Laser cutters / CNC | Training required; ventilation matters |
| DNA / enzymes | Institutional biosafety rules; not a bedroom project |
| Budget for 10 magnetic modules | Often tens to low hundreds of USD in parts |
| Budget for 50+ electronic modules | Quickly thousands once PCBs, batteries, and labor count |
| Budget for DNA origami experiment | Dominated by oligos + lab access + imaging time |

## Try this (manufacturing mini-plan)

Write a one-page plan for a **5-module** magnetic tile system:

1. Sketch faces and polarity.
2. List print settings + magnet size.
3. Estimate cost from [Supply chain](/supply-chain) vendors.
4. Define a pass/fail test (“forms a chain of 5 within 60 s of agitation”).
